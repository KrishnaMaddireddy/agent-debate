A policy argument is only as good as its comparison. Cover each of these, briefly, and name the ones the other seats skipped.

1. **Compared with what?** State the realistic alternative (including doing nothing). "X is good" means nothing without "X is better than Y for this goal".
2. **Goal and metric.** Say what success would look like in a number someone could measure in five years, and who gets to measure it.
3. **Who pays, who benefits, who decides.** Distribution matters as much as the total. Note concentrated costs on a few versus diffuse benefits for many, and the reverse.
4. **Second-order effects.** Substitution (people switch to the untaxed thing), enforcement cost, and what the policy signals to others.
5. **Reversibility and timing.** Can it be undone if it fails? Does the payoff arrive on the timescale the goal requires?
6. **Precedent.** Has a comparable place tried it? What happened, and how comparable is it really?

Separate empirical claims (a study could settle) from value judgements (only priorities can settle), and label which kind each of your points is.
